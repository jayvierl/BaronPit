# Independent challenge to the match-discussion pilot

Reviewed November 27, 2026 against `origin/main` at `0cecdc0` and the local `feature/contextual-match-discussions` worktree. The review used the repository and isolated fixtures. Nothing was sent to production.

## Product judgment

**Run a small player pilot. A scripted exchange establishes that the interaction works. It cannot establish demand, retention, or the supply of helpful peers.** A visitor who has just looked up a match can ask about one decision with the relevant result attached. Another player can examine the same game, answer, and bring the original asker back to that exact reply. Each person gets something even if they never publish a second post. This is an exchange worth testing for a social product that also hopes to help people find good teammates.

Most of the machinery was already on main. Match-specific question starters, public player lookup, champion rooms, replies, private saves, following, thread permalinks, and reply Activity already existed. The branch joins those pieces with a visible match-level entry point, a preview that says whether the author has verified ownership of the displayed Riot ID, an explicit publishing choice, and a return link to the selected match. Crediting the entire interaction as a new platform would make it harder to tell what actually improved.

## The smallest credible alternative

Use the existing match question editor and champion hub. Prefill the specific match, keep the author's text editable, show who and what will be public, and send the resulting reply to existing Activity. This preserves the principal benefit with one writer flow and one reply system. A separate stream for match commentary would split a small supply of replies. A useful answer helps someone decide whom to follow. Testing the broader duo promise requires observing whether two willing players actually queued together and whether both wanted another game. An answer, a save, and a follow cannot answer that. Keep the existing private Duo Finder invitation as the path between those outcomes. Test the additional perspective option in the same conversation system. Give help-seeking questions their own queue so someone volunteering an answer can find a player who actually asked for one.

## What to keep private and cheap

The attachment resolver rechecks a bounded Riot snapshot. It identifies verified ownership server-side, rejects unsupported queues, and projects selected public fields instead of copying a full match payload into the public card. The feed applies viewer-specific hiding and moderation before returning content. The post intentionally persists as social content and keeps a server-side source reference. Avoid adding public participant rosters to the post, third-party image trackers, game archives, per-profile view histories, or push/email broadcasts simply to measure this loop.

Two qualitative checks cost less than another ranking feature. Ask a reader what “Player ownership unverified” means before following the match link. Ask the original writer which part of the reply helped after their next game. A dashboard count cannot answer either question.

For staffing, count new-account submissions separately from ordinary reports. If 30 first-day posts and replies require two minutes each, that alone consumes one reviewer-hour that day. This is a planning example. Replace both inputs with measured queue volume and service time, including evenings and weekends. For infrastructure, inspect an ordinary public match preview and a high-activity champion feed under representative load before promising latency or cost. This branch's lookup reads the existing snapshot; adding a separate replay-fetch service would change that cost model substantially.

## Changes observed during the review

The preview now names the Riot ID on the card, distinguishes verified ownership from an unverified relationship, and explains that public match facts persist after the match leaves recent history. Held writers are directed to My submissions. The old one-click share button has been removed from match history, and the general post endpoint returns HTTP 400 for a new card submitted there. New match attachments go through the public confirmation flow. Public posting and reply aggregates now include unlinked authors, while the dashboard labels the day-seven cohort separately. The unanswered-question query excludes perspectives. Roleless match previews use Team and a teamfight prompt, removing the requirement to invent a lane. These changes address concrete weaknesses without adding another social destination.

I independently ran the auth, discussion-library, decision, paginated-feed, and match-discussion-metrics suites. All 30 tests passed after the public-queue fixture corrections.

I then ran all eight browser checks against the local 01:29 UTC production build. A guest used a keyboard to find a match. An unlinked participant returned to an editable draft after signing in, acknowledged the public attachment, and posted. A separate participant found the discussion in both the feed and champion hub, saved it privately, followed the writer and replied. The writer opened that precise reply from Activity and returned to the same match; KDA, result, item links, time and rank display survived a reload. The final checks confirmed viewer-specific mute and block behavior and author withdrawal. The script also checked the guest view with JavaScript disabled and found no browser errors across its two main sessions. These checks use synthetic Riot records and authenticated local fixtures. Public email delivery, first-day moderation turnaround, willingness to return and a subsequent duo game still require real participants.
